This part of my portfolio is my evidence of working in an Agile team on GoShuttle, a student transport initiative that partners CPUT with Golden Arrow Bus Service to run dedicated, subsidised bus routes for students who struggle with unreliable transport.

I was the team’s Researcher. I drafted our pre-registration Google Form, led the MoSCoW prioritisation of our backlog, and built the fleet and live-tracking tables and the driver and student tracking screens.

It follows four steps. I collected artefacts from the project, selected the ones that best show my learning, reflected on each, and connected them into one account of how our team planned and delivered its work. Each section below has the evidence first and my reflection after it.

Reflection

As the Researcher on the GoShuttle team, I looked into what an Agile team charter is and why it is important at the start of a project. An Agile team charter is a kind of agreement between the team members and the stakeholders. Stakeholders are the people who benefit from the project’s success. The charter is created at the beginning of the project to agree on the team’s purpose, goals, values, roles, working agreements and how the team will operate. I shared my findings with the team so we could create our own charter. My role is even written into the charter: if a team vote ends in a tie, I gather information within 48 hours so we can vote again. That means my research directly helps with how we make decisions.

Before this, I thought a charter was a document that said how the project would be done. Now I know it is a living agreement that sets expectations early, before problems happen. The part that helped me the most was the Definition of Done. When we were writing it, we knew some parts of the project might change because of things we could not predict. So we only included what we were certain would be finished, and some planned work did change. Our Kanban board on Trello, the Monday and Wednesday stand-up meetings and the Friday review meeting showed me that planning as a group is better than planning alone. It helps us talk about what we will do each week and see what others are working on, so our tasks don’t overlap. These meetings made me more productive and helped keep everyone responsible.

What worked well was that team members were able to finish their work on time, even though we had trouble assigning work. Some members were not available on some days because of their own commitments. That made it hard to plan work, because we could not be sure if someone would be able to finish their part. That led to some members taking on extra tasks.

The charter helped us when we faced challenges or disagreements. For example, our agreement to “raise blockers early” helped when someone could not complete their work or was stuck on something, because they could speak up before the work was delayed. Our risk section also said we might get fewer than 200 survey responses, and that actually happened. That showed me that setting expectations helps you plan ahead and reduce the impact when things go wrong.

Next time I would review the charter every week before our meetings, not just at the start. That would remind us of our commitments and help stop conflicts from starting. I would also remind everyone to do the same, because people can forget their responsibilities during a project. I would also avoid taking over other people’s responsibilities, because that can lead to one person carrying too much.

The charter connects to my logbook because the logbook gives a breakdown of what the charter says should be done in the project. The sprint backlog breaks it down more into what should be done each week.

Reflection

Our backlog was an ordered list of everything GoShuttle might need, kept on our Trello board. On 21 July the team turned the features from our MVP week into fourteen user stories. I led the discussion where we prioritised them using MoSCoW. By the end of the day the Must Have column held the six highest-value stories for our first design sprint. We also found that some stories were too broad, so we split the “Book a trip” story into three cards (select route and time, confirm fare, and receive confirmation) so that progress would be easier to see.

The order of the backlog came from student feedback. On campus, students asked how our pricing compared to taxis and whether a pass could be paid with NSFAS funds, and several were unsure about trusting a new service. Those concerns directly shaped which stories we marked Must Have. They are why the backlog includes items such as the Payments page and NSFAS allowance direction. This was an example of feedback feeding the backlog.

The backlog also showed our progress. The research cards, such as Interview Questions, Survey Design, Problem Definition and Solution Proposal, were moved to Done, while the remaining items, from the Stops and Routes page in Sprint 8 to NSFAS allowance direction in Sprint 12, stayed in the backlog for later sprints.

Leading the prioritisation taught me to put aside what I thought the users needed and to put the users’ preferences first.

Next time I would add acceptance criteria to each card, because some cards had work that was not properly complete. Acceptance criteria would help us go back to the cards that did not meet them.

Having Trello open while we coded showed us which parts of the flow were still missing so we didn’t over-polish one screen while others lagged behind.

The sprint that taught me the most was Database Design. In the Database Design sprint we drew the entity relationship diagram. Defined the relationships between the entities and I built the Bus, Driver and LiveTrip tables. I learned that planning the data before building the screens is essential. It helps avoid problems and makes development smoother.

Our plans also changed during the sprints and we responded instead of waiting. Our fare logic was unclear so we hard-coded an estimate. Labelled it "illustrative”. This taught me that when planning for a sprint you must be ready and open to changes that may come.

Next time I would update checklists daily or plan sprints around who is available all week

Business Model Canvas, logo direction, solution architecture and the HTML redesign conversion.

Blockers / decisions: Some architecture decisions (how much to hard-code vs prepare for future backend) were unclear. Decision: keep the current build frontend-focused and document future integration points clearly.

Kwanda and Sinethemba completed a clearer solution architecture outline and advanced the HTML conversion for the Confirmation and basic Tracking placeholder pages.

Blockers / decisions: Converting the redesign revealed some inconsistent spacing and mobile issues. Decision: prioritise mobile-first adjustments while converting, rather than leaving them for later.

Reflection: Seeing the business model, logo and technical structure develop side-by- side made the project feel more coherent. The logo work benefited directly from the clearer value proposition.

Day 4 — Tuesday, 07 July

Focus: Integration review + Documentation close-out

Planned: Cross-review the Business Model Canvas, preferred logo direction, solution architecture and converted web pages. Capture decisions and prepare for the next phase (MVP design & launch).

Trello / task tracking was updated with completed items.

Blockers / decisions: We still lacked real user validation for parts of the business model. Decision: treat the current canvas as a living draft and test key assumptions (pricing, trust, channels) during the upcoming MVP week.

Reflection: Closing the week with a joint review helped the two pairs understand each other’s work. Moving from pure design/architecture into validated learning next week feels like the natural Agile next step.

14 to 17 July: MVP launch and campus activation

WhatsApp flyer, Google Form and an info table at the Bellville campus.

Read the full logbook

Day 1 — Monday, 14 July

Focus: MVP Day 1 — Design & launch (part 1)

Planned: Design the WhatsApp flyer, draft the four-question Google Form, review copy against our 5W messaging, and list the ten target CPUT WhatsApp groups.

Actual: Sinethemba completed the flyer design by midday. Mvuyisi drafted the Google Form but paused halfway through when we realized we hadn't confirmed the final order of home-area options. We agreed to use Khayelitsha, Mitchells Plain, Delft, and Kraaifontein, matching the refined target market from Improvement 1. Kwanda managed to confirm access to eight of the ten target WhatsApp groups; two group admins had not yet approved our join requests by end of day.

Blockers / decisions: Two WhatsApp groups were still pending admin approval. We decided to proceed with the eight confirmed groups on 15 July and add the remaining two once access was granted, rather than delay the whole launch.

Reflection: We should have requested access to the WhatsApp groups earlier in the week rather than on the same day we needed them — gatekeeper approvals take time we didn't account for in our planning.

Day 2 — Tuesday, 15 July

Focus: MVP Day 1–2 — Design & launch (part 2)

Planned: Finalize and post the flyer and Google Form link to the ten WhatsApp groups, track posting progress, and begin logging pre-registrations.

Actual: We posted to all eight confirmed groups by 10am. By early afternoon the form had only around six responses, which was slower than we expected. Samukelisiwe reached out to other WhatsApp groups . Responses picked up noticeably afterward, ending the day at 14 pre-registrations, mostly from Khayelitsha.

Blockers / decisions: Organic WhatsApp sharing alone was not generating responses fast enough. We decided to reach out to other WhatsApp groups that had number.

Reflection: Posting on student WhatsApp groups made a real, measurable difference to response rate. In future phases we should involve the SRC from day one rather than treating it as a backup channel.

Day 3 — Wednesday, 16 July

Focus: MVP Day 3 — Campus activation (part 1)

Planned: Print flyers with the Google Form QR code, set up an info table at the CPUT Bellville campus entrance, hand out flyers, and record student feedback.

Actual: The table ran from 8am to 12pm at the Bellville entrance. We distributed roughly 120 printed flyers. Several students stopped to ask questions, most commonly about pricing compared to taxis and whether the pass could be paid using NSFAS funds. A few students were visibly skeptical of a new, unbranded transport service.

Blockers / decisions: Trust was a recurring concern among students we spoke to..

Reflection: Naming the golden arrow backing upfront visibly changed how open students were to signing up. This is a messaging lesson we will carry into all future outreach, not just campus activation.

Day 4 — Thursday, 17 July

Focus: MVP Day 4 — Campus activation (part 2) + interim check-in

Planned: Continue campus activation, compile the interim pre-registration count and home-area spread, cross-check against our MVP success targets, and decide whether to bring forward the social media push.

Actual: We ran the table again from 8am to 11am. By the end of the day total pre-registrations reached 41, spread across three confirmed home areas (Khayelitsha, Mitchells Plain, Delft). About 35% of respondents indicated willingness to pay R20 or more per trip.

Blockers / decisions: We were behind two of our five success metrics — total sign-ups (41 vs. a target of 50) and willingness to pay (35% vs. a target of 40%). As a team we decided to bring forward the TikTok/Instagram comparison video originally scheduled for Days 5–6, to try to close the gap before the seven-day test window closes.

Reflection: Falling slightly short of two targets midway through the test wasn't a failure — it was useful information. Adjusting our schedule in response, rather than waiting passively for the original plan to play out, felt like the clearest example this week of actually working in an Agile way rather than just calling our process Agile.

Summary of learning

Across this stretch, the biggest lessons were about timing and trust rather than the product itself. Gatekeeper-dependent tasks (WhatsApp group access) needed longer lead times than we planned for, and leading with our golden arrow partnership consistently outperformed leading with the product features when talking to students. Both insights fed directly into our decision to bring the social media phase forward rather than sticking rigidly to the original seven-day sequence — the kind of responsive adjustment the Agile approach is meant to support.

Reflection

Keeping the Agile logbook meant writing down each day what we planned what actually happened, our blockers and decisions and a short reflection. From 4 July to the end of August the logbook recorded our work on the business model and architecture the launch of the MVP and campus activation the set‑up of Trello and wireframes the coded interface and the switch to a Supabase backend. Planning the day was what kept me motivated and helped me stay productive.

The logbook shows that we change the plan instead of just following it. On 17 July we were behind on two of our five success measures with 41 pre‑registrations of 50 and 35 % willingness to pay instead of 40 %. So we moved the TikTok and Instagram video forward. On 29 and 30 July we hard‑coded an "fare estimate and used a static map image so the prototype could finish and be tested. In August we moved from Spring Boot to Supabase because GitHub Pages cannot run a Java backend. Changing a plan taught me that there is no risk in applying plans as long as I am well informed.

My own part in these weeks was drafting the pre‑registration Google Form on 14 July leading the MoSCoW prioritisation on 21 July and in August building the Bus, Driver and LiveTrip tables, DriverTrack.vue and TrackView.vue. That work taught me the importance of data structure and the value of early testing.

The logbook was linked to our Trello board and, to the charter. Cards were moved to Done on Trello at the end of each sprint. Recorded in the logbook and the "Blockers / decisions" entry gave us a place to raise problems early which was one of our working agreements. This worked well because it helped us when we could not access WhatsApp groups.

It shows that reviewing work while it is still happening helps you catch problems early before they become issues. When we only noticed the WhatsApp group access delay and the weak end‑of‑day sync afterward it was already too late to fix them. Checking progress often during the week would have let us adjust the plan in time instead of only learning the lesson after the damage was done.

Next time I would write the logbook at the end of each day instead of in batches because it would help us notice blockers and coordination problems while we still have time to fix them.

Overall Reflection

This section connects the five artefacts into one story of how our team worked.

Reflection

The five items in this portfolio describe one process. The charter set out how team would work, including the roles, the Monday and Wednesday stand‑ups, the Friday meetings and the agreement to raise blockers early. The user stories described what students and drivers needed and became the cards in the product backlog. Team prioritized that backlog with MoSCoW and the sprint backlog on Trello turned it into the work for each sprint. The logbook then recorded what actually happened each day. These items depended on each other: the charter created the rules the user stories and backlog turned those rules into work. The logbook showed whether team actually followed the plan and where team needed to adjust.

Overall working on GoShuttle taught me that Agile teamwork works best when team agrees the rules early and keeps checking progress instead of working in isolation. Planning together in the stand‑ups and Friday meetings helped team avoid clashing tasks. Kept everyone accountable, which is something team would not have achieved by working alone.

There were places where team worked in an Agile way instead of only saying team did. On 17 July team was behind on two of five success measures so team brought the media video forward instead of waiting for the original plan. This showed me that responding to feedback and changing the plan quickly is more valuable than sticking to a schedule that is no longer working.

There were also places where team fell short. Work was not always evenly spread because some members were unavailable, on some days, which created uncertainty when assigning tasks and sometimes left work for fewer people. This taught me that raising blockers early is not enough if team does not also check availability properly when planning the week.

In my project I will review the team agreement every week and write the logbook at the end of each day because these two habits will help team notice problems earlier and keep the whole team aligned while there is still time to fix them.
